My Personal Theory of Instructional Design

What I believe about how people learn, and how that belief shapes the way I design.

Course

EME 602 Theories of Learning and Cognition for Instruction, with Dr. Gunce Malan-Rush, Fall 2026

Conditions

Individual work. I wrote this theory for my portfolio in November 2026, building on my EME 602 coursework: five theory papers, an annotated bibliography, class discussions, and my theory presentation.

Scope

My beliefs about learning, five design principles grounded in learning theory and research, examples from my own projects, and the questions my theory still leaves open.

My Role

Sole author.

This theory is also available as an APA-style PDF.

My theory in one sentence

People learn when new ideas connect to what they already know, arrive in amounts their working memory can handle, and are put to work on real tasks with support and feedback, until they can do the work on their own.

Where my theory comes from

For me, memorizing has never been enough to call something learned. Real learning means understanding an idea well enough to connect it to what I already know and then use it somewhere that matters. In EME 602, I found that this view fits pragmatism, which treats knowledge as something built through experience and reasoning, with a piece of interpretivism: two people can face the same situation and understand it differently because of what each one brings to it (Driscoll, 2005).

My theory also reflects where I come from. Before instructional design, I trained more than 100 end users on ERP software in real workplaces, and most of the examples in my theory papers are workplace examples: a new employee learning a spreadsheet, a staff member learning how company files are organized, a trainee processing customer refunds. My theory is built for people who need to use what they learn.

No single theory explains all of that, so I combine several. Cognitive information processing explains how information gets into memory and stays there. Schema theory, constructivism, and situated cognition explain how it becomes meaningful and usable. Interactional theories and research on motivation explain why support and confidence matter. Gagné's theory of instruction ties these ideas into a plan: clear objectives, a sequence of instructional events, practice, feedback, and transfer to real situations (Driscoll, 2005).

Five principles I design by

1

Start from what learners already know

The theory

Learners are never blank slates. New information sticks when it connects to an existing schema, and inaccurate prior knowledge can just as easily lead learners astray, so a designer needs to know what learners bring before designing anything (Driscoll, 2005).

In my work

In EME 601, my partner and I began with a needs analysis built on survey data from 40 educators. In Beginner's Guide to Supply Chain Processes, I designed for recent graduates with limited industry experience, so the course introduces the supply chain and each department's responsibilities before it walks through step-by-step procedures.

2

Respect the limits of attention and memory

The theory

Attention is the gate to memory, and working memory holds only a handful of new items for a short time (Miller, 1956, as cited in Driscoll, 2005). Chunking content, directing attention, connecting new ideas to familiar ones, and pairing words with pictures all help information reach long-term memory (Driscoll, 2005).

In my work

In Leading with Emotional Intelligence, our team used short, chunked lessons in plain language to reduce cognitive load. My EME 602 presentation on cognitive information processing practiced what it taught: one main idea per slide, color to direct attention, and a two-round activity in which classmates first saw how much they missed and then used three memory strategies to remember more.

3

Make learning real

The theory

Knowledge becomes usable when learners apply it in realistic situations, through simulations, case studies, and authentic tasks (Driscoll, 2005). Constructivist writers make the same case for problem solving and hands-on work, especially in career-focused learning (Bada, 2015; Doolittle & Camp, 1999), and Merrill's (2002) first principles put a real-world task at the center of instruction.

In my work

The Disaster Response Simulator places learners in a story-driven hurricane scenario where every decision has consequences. Professional Email Communication for Workplace Success uses realistic client requests, and every lesson in Designing for Motivation and Engagement works toward a real teaching task.

4

Support learners, then step back

The theory

Learners can do more with guidance than on their own, and that support should fade as their skills grow (Driscoll, 2005). Feedback does two jobs: it tells learners whether they are right, and it gives them what they need to fix their mistakes. Confidence and self-regulation matter too, because learners who believe they can succeed put in more effort, and they grow more independent as they learn to set goals, monitor their progress, and reflect (Driscoll, 2005; Zimmerman, 2002).

In my work

In the Disaster Response Simulator, an expert guide explains the rules, and immediate feedback explains why each choice is safe or unsafe. Beginner's Guide to Supply Chain Processes works as an on-the-job guide that new employees can open alongside their daily work, and Leading with Emotional Intelligence builds in reflection prompts.

5

Design for every learner, and keep checking

The theory

Learners differ in language, culture, background, and experience, and those differences shape both their motivation and their understanding (Driscoll, 2005). That is why I treat a design as unfinished until real learners have used it, a view I explored further when I presented think-aloud observations in EME 660.

In my work

Our EME 603 study found that captions and color contrast helped English language learners understand a lesson, and it also showed that accessibility alone does not guarantee engagement. In EME 601, a formative survey and a think-aloud session led my partner and me to trim our course, reorganize it, and add a hands-on activity.

What my theory leaves open

Cognitive information processing treats knowledge as something learners take in and store, while constructivism and situated cognition see it as something they build in context. I use both: the first helps me decide how much to show and when, and the second helps me decide what learners should do with it. My EME 603 research added one more caution: making a lesson accessible does not automatically make it engaging, so I pair inclusive design with interactivity and real tasks. Because I see knowledge as something that changes with new experience, I expect this theory to keep changing as I design for new learners and measure the results.
